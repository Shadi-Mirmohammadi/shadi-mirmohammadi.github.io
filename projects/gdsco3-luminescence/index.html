    Excitation-Switchable Eu³⁺ Luminescence in GdScO₃

    tune the UV excitation and the emission color changes scroll to run the experiment ↓

    Seyedeh Shadi Mirmohammadi, Afrouz Taherian, Yunshan Wang, Diego P. Fernandez, Michael A. Scarpulla, Steve Blair, and Berardi Sensale-Rodriguez University of Utah

    Manuscript in preparation for Optical Materials Express (2026).

    Visible emission from a nominally undoped substrate

    GdScO₃ is a perovskite substrate used for growing epitaxial oxide films. It is chosen for its lattice match to titanates, not for its optical properties. The substrates we studied are commercial, nominally undoped single crystals from two vendors (Stonylab (100) and CrysTec (110)). Under deep-UV excitation they show bright, sharp visible emission.

    The emission comes from europium at the parts-per-million level, an unintentional trace impurity thousands of times below deliberate doping, roughly one Eu³⁺ ion per 10⁵–10⁶ Gd³⁺. Such a small trace emits strongly because of the host: the crystal is a dense lattice of optically active Gd³⁺, and energy absorbed anywhere in the Gd³⁺ sublattice migrates to the Eu³⁺ sites.

    Emission color versus excitation wavelength

    Using the tunable deep-UV source (third-harmonic generation of a Ti:sapphire laser) that I built for my GeO₂ photodetector measurements, we swept the excitation from 236 to 270 nm and recorded the emission at each step. The emission channel switches continuously with the excitation wavelength. At short excitation wavelengths the emission is dominated by the higher-lying Eu³⁺ ⁵D₂/⁵D₁ lines (491 nm and the 535–555 nm group). When the excitation is tuned into the Gd³⁺ ⁶IJ absorption near 266 nm, that upper manifold collapses and the emission is almost pure red ⁵D₀ → ⁷F₁,₂ at 590 and 612 nm.

    The switch survives calibration. Re-plotting the same map with the emission normalized to the absorbed light and to the excitation power (using a NIST-traceable lamp calibration of the spectrometer) makes this clearer: pumping the Gd³⁺ ⁶IJ resonance near 266 nm is by far the most efficient way to excite the red Eu³⁺ lines.

    The switch is quantitative. The intensity ratio I₄₉₁/I₆₁₂ falls by more than an order of magnitude across the sweep, crossing unity near 251 nm, and its descending branch follows a single exponential (k = 0.124 nm⁻¹, an e-folding every ~8 nm). In CIE color coordinates the emission moves continuously from yellow-green to orange:

    A single, nominally undoped crystal that changes its visible emission color through the excitation wavelength alone, with no change in composition, is of interest for optical tagging and anti-counterfeiting and for ratiometric luminescence sensing. Here we used it as a rapid, nondestructive probe of the host’s energy-transfer pathways.

    Identifying the emitter

    Sharp lines at 491, 590, and 612 nm could in principle come from several rare-earth ions, so we measured the full trace-impurity content of both crystals by LA-ICP-MS:

    The argument is by elimination. The two crystals have nearly disjoint impurity fingerprints but show the same sharp-line emission at identical wavelengths, so the emitter must be an impurity they share. Only Dy and Eu are visible-active in both, and Dy³⁺ would emit at 482 and 575 nm in this host, which we never see. The line positions match Eu-doped GdScO₃ from the literature, and the excitation behavior matches Gd³⁺ → Eu³⁺ sensitization.

    Screening substrates with light

    The two crystals differ most in the broad defect-emission band, which is strong in one, nearly absent in the other, and uncorrelated with the measured impurity content. Annealing one crystal at 1200 °C in air suppresses this band almost completely while the sharp Eu³⁺ lines survive, which identifies it as an intrinsic, annealing-suppressible defect feature. The anneal also raises the Eu³⁺ ⁷F₂/⁷F₁ asymmetry ratio from 1.30 to 1.90, so the trace Eu itself reports the improvement in its local environment.

    X-ray diffraction before and after annealing

    X-ray diffraction gives a structural check on the anneal. A rocking curve measures the spread in orientation of the crystal planes, and a narrow peak means a low-mosaicity, well-ordered crystal. After the anneal the peak is much sharper:

    The peak is 2.6× narrower and 2.8× brighter after the anneal. Two independent probes, one optical and one structural, point to the same conclusion, which makes the defect-band assignment credible.

    The paper combines excitation-selective photoluminescence with trace-element analysis and annealing to demonstrate a fast, nondestructive optical screen for scandate substrates. UV illumination of a wafer gives an emission spectrum that reports both its energy-transfer pathways and its defect signatures. This is relevant to substrate qualification for oxide electronics and to rare-earth scandates as excitation-tunable luminescent hosts.
