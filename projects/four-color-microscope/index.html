    Optical Design of a Four-Color Fluorescence Microscope

    Shadi Mirmohammadi, Electrical & Computer Engineering, University of Utah, in the lab of Prof. Steve Blair.
    Design in progress, November 2026.

    01

    The imaging path

    I am designing a four-color fluorescence microscope for histology, built from catalog parts in Prof. Steve Blair's lab. Its imaging path is an objective, a filter cube with a 45° dichroic, a 150 mm achromatic doublet tube lens and a 12 MP camera with 3.45 µm pixels. I learned Zemax OpticStudio for this design and built the path in it one element at a time.

    With the lab's 10x/0.25 objective, made for a 160 mm tube, and the tube lens 70 mm above it, the camera sits 45.694 mm past the tube lens. The magnification is 6.49x, one picture covers 2.18 × 1.60 mm of the sample, and the RMS spot radius on the camera is 0.265 µm in the middle, 3.636 µm at 0.85 mm off-axis and 9.045 µm in the image corner.

    A 160 mm objective sends converging light, so the tube lens focuses it much sooner than the 142.975 mm where it focuses parallel light. That number came from the first step, the tube lens alone with an 18 mm beam at 520 nm: effective focal length 149.93 mm, RMS spot radius 0.48, 16.0 and 28.6 µm at 0, 2.43 and 3.34°, Airy radius 5.29 µm. The 70 mm spacing is an estimate until the column is assembled.

    02

    Zemax against Python

    Zemax was new to me, so I traced the same system in Python and compared every step before adding the next element. The Python model sends real rays through the four tube-lens surfaces and the tilted dichroic. The PSF, Strehl ratio and MTF come from an FFT of the wavefront error over the pupil.

    Across the first six steps the two models agree within 3%. The largest difference is the Strehl ratio in the image corner behind the glass window, 0.354 in Zemax against 0.344 in Python. The focus positions agree to 1 µm.

    Zemax's standard spot diagram traces 127 rays in a hexapolar pattern, so its RMS radius comes out larger than the true value: 0.48 µm against 0.26 µm on axis for the tube lens alone. The Python model traces the same 127 rays for these comparisons. Zemax's paraxial lens bends rays by the tangent rule, and so does the Python stand-in for the objective.

    03

    Four colors, one focus

    The four emission colors, 461, 520, 570 and 670 nm, reach the camera through the same tube lens. An achromatic doublet brings only two wavelengths to exactly the same focus, so I checked how far the other colors fall from it and how far apart their images land.

    From 461 to 670 nm the focus moves by 13.5 µm at the camera, while the depth of focus there is ±164 µm, so all four colors share one camera position. In the image corner the four images land within 3.6 µm of each other, about one 3.45 µm camera pixel.

    Both models treat the objective as a perfect lens, so these numbers belong to the tube lens; the real objective adds its own color error. The depth of focus is ±λ/(2 NA²) with the image-side NA of 0.040, which is ±4.2 µm at the sample.

    04

    Sharpness across the field

    The Strehl ratio, the peak of the real point image relative to a perfect one, shows what each added element costs. I added the 45° dichroic and then a 0.7 mm glass window in front of the sample, and refocused the camera on the middle of the field at each step.

    Objective and tube lens alone: Strehl 1.0 / 0.844 / 0.396 in the middle, at 0.85 mm and in the corner, and an MTF of 0.584 at 50 cycles/mm in the middle. With the dichroic: 0.952 / 0.712 / 0.304. Through the glass window: 0.906 / 0.665 / 0.354.

    Strehl 0.8 is the usual mark for a diffraction-limited image. The tilted plate sits in converging light here, so it adds astigmatism even in the middle of the field. In the Python model a 0.45 NA objective without a correction collar falls to Strehl 0.04 behind the same glass, so the final objective needs a collar that can be set for the window.

    05

    The 10x/0.5 objective

    Next I loaded the maker's black-box Zemax file of a 10x/0.5 infinity objective, the higher-NA candidate, and kept the same tube lens. Its pupil sits 45 mm inside the objective. Beams from off-axis points pivot about that pupil and land far from the center of the tube lens, 115 mm above it.

    Magnification 7.49x. RMS spot radius referred to the sample: 0.78 µm in the middle, 2.5 µm at 0.85 mm and 5.1 µm in the image corner, against an Airy radius of 0.63 µm. The tube lens limits the field edge: with a perfect objective instead of the real one, the Python model still predicts 2.3 and 4.6 µm at the same points. Off-axis beams land off-center on the 20 mm opening of the tube lens, the corner beam by 6.7 mm, so about 30% of the light is lost at 0.85 mm and 40% in the corner.

    Light through the opening from the Python model, at 0, 0.35, 0.58, 0.85 and 1.17 mm from the middle: 99, 87, 79, 69 and 58%. The image corner is at 1.17 mm here because the magnification is higher. Two changes would help: a shorter distance from the objective to the tube lens, which brings the edge light back, and a larger, better-corrected tube lens, which also reduces the edge blur.

    06

    Role and status

    My role
    I set up each step in Zemax OpticStudio 2025 R2 (sequential mode), from the tube lens alone to the maker's objective file, and checked each one against the Python ray trace before going on.
    Status
    Design in progress, November 2026. The 70 mm spacing between objective and tube lens is an estimate; once the column is assembled I will measure it and rerun both models.
    Tools
    Zemax OpticStudio: catalog and black-box lens files, paraxial lenses, tilted plates, Quick Focus, spot diagrams, FFT PSF and MTF. Python: real-ray tracing through the tube lens and the dichroic, wavefront and FFT analysis.
